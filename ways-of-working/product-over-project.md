# Product over project

## What it is

A project has a start date, an end date, a budget and a list of things to deliver. A product has users, outcomes and no end date; it lives as long as it is worth keeping. "Product over project" is the shift from asking "did we deliver what was specified, on time and on budget?" to asking "did the thing we built change anything for the people who use it?". It draws on the product-management writing of Marty Cagan (*Inspired*, *Empowered*), the lean startup work of Eric Ries, Jeff Patton's *User Story Mapping*, and Mik Kersten's *Project to Product*.

## Why it matters with agents in the loop

An agent can produce output at a pace that makes output the wrong thing to count. If the plan counts pages written, features built or tickets closed, an agent will happily generate all three, and the organisation will be busy and no better off. The question that matters moves from "can we produce this?" to "should we, and did it help?". That is a product question.

Agents also make experiments cheap. A prototype that took two weeks can take two hours. Teams that think in outcomes can use that to try more ideas and discard more of them. Teams that think in projects use the same speed to deliver the original plan faster, including the parts that were never a good idea.

## The practice

### Outcomes over output

An **output** is a thing made: a report, a feature, a deck. An **outcome** is a change in what people do or what the organisation gets: fewer repair-status calls, more renewals, faster onboarding. Write the outcome first and measure it against a baseline taken at Scope. Output is a means to it.

A useful test: if the output ships and the measure does not move, was the work a success? If you answer yes, you are counting output.

### Long-lived teams

Keep a team together around a product or service so it builds knowledge of the users, the data and the history. Disbanding a team at the end of a project throws that knowledge away and starts the next team from nothing. Moving the work to the team is cheaper than moving the team to the work.

### Fund teams, not projects

Give a standing team a budget and a set of outcomes to improve, and let it decide the work, instead of approving each project by business case. Funding by project forces long-range guesses about what will work, then rewards sticking to the guess. Funding a team lets the plan change as evidence arrives. Review the funding on evidence at a regular interval (for example every quarter), and stop funding what is not working.

### Discovery and delivery together

**Discovery** is finding out what is worth building: talking to users, testing ideas, prototyping. **Delivery** is building it properly. Teams that separate them, with one group deciding and another building, lose the learning. Run both in the same team, in the same weeks: a few hours of discovery every week, not a phase that ends.

### Hypotheses, not requirements

Write ideas as testable bets: "We believe that X will result in Y, and we will know because Z changes." Decide in advance what result would make you stop. Cheap tests first: a conversation, a mock-up, a manual version of the service before automating it. This is the Hypothesise stage.

### The product owner's role

The product owner is accountable for the outcomes and decides what the team works on next. They:

- Hold the problem statement and keep it free of solutions.
- Say yes or no to the acceptance for each piece of work.
- Own the priorities, and the reasons for them, in writing.
- Talk to users regularly, and bring what they learn to the team.
- Say what is out of scope and keep to it.

They are not a ticket writer or a messenger from the sponsor. In a small team the owner may also be a builder, but the decision about what is accepted must have one named person.

*With agents:* the owner is the person who accepts the agent's output. An agent can help the owner by drafting options, summarising user feedback and preparing comparisons. It does not decide priorities and it does not accept its own work.

## How SHOTos supports it

- **Hypothesise** makes every piece of work a testable bet with a measure, before building starts.
- **Track** compares the result with the baseline and asks for a named person's decision to move to Own, pivot, stop or hold for evidence. Stopping is a valid, recorded result.
- **Own and Sustain** build a long-lived team that can keep the product going and carry the method to the next team.
- The [capture-the-problem skill](../skills/capture-the-problem/README.md) and [problem template](../templates/problem.md) keep the starting point a problem, not a solution.

## What good looks like

- Every piece of work names an outcome and a way to tell if it moved.
- A baseline exists before the first change.
- A team has a standing mission, not an end date.
- Ideas are cheap to try and cheap to drop, and dropped ones are recorded with the reason.
- The sponsor asks "what did we learn?" as often as "what did we ship?".
- Users appear in the team's week.

## Anti-patterns

- **The feature factory.** Success is measured by the number of releases.
- **The handover cliff.** A project team builds it, then leaves; a different team inherits something they never saw.
- **A requirements document as a contract.** Treating the first guess as fixed.
- **Output dashboards.** Pages, tickets and lines counted as progress.
- **A product owner with no authority** to say no.
- **Discovery as a phase** that finishes before building begins and never returns.
- **No stop rule.** Work that nobody is allowed to end.

## How it maps to the stages and rules

- **Scope:** problem statement and baseline.
- **Hypothesise:** bets with measures; discovery in small steps.
- **Operate:** deliver the smallest piece into real use.
- **Track:** the outcome measured; the decision recorded.
- **Own and Sustain:** keep the team and carry the practice forward.
- **Rule 2:** acceptance is written in terms of the outcome.
- **Rule 4:** claim an outcome only with a measure, labelled estimate or actual.

## A short example

Harbourside Lettings has a request: "Build a tenant portal." Under a project mindset, the plan lists twelve features and a deadline. Under a product mindset, the team starts with the problem: tenants phone the office to ask about repair status, and the office takes about fifty such calls a week (an invented number for this example). The hypothesis: a weekly automatic status message will cut those calls by half within six weeks. The team builds a manual version first, with the agent drafting the messages and an office manager checking and sending them. After three weeks the calls have fallen by about a third, and tenants ask for photos. The owner decides to keep going with automatic sending for one repair type only, and funds the team for another quarter on the evidence. The portal is not built, and nobody misses it.
