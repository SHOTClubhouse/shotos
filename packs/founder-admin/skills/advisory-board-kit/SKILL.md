# Advisory board kit

Turn a loose group of helpful people into a board with clear roles, expectations and limits, without making promises the company cannot keep.

Stage: Hypothesise. Rules served: 1 (stop at the boundaries), 2 (agree acceptance first) and 4 (claim only what you can show).

## Context and handoff

Read the purpose, proposed members, relevant expertise and existing governance. Separate advisory input from approval authority.

Use `advisory-session` when the group is convened. Keep mandate, membership and terms with the board record.

## When not to use this

Skip it for a formal board of directors (a legal body with duties; ask a lawyer), for a one-off favour from a friend, and for deciding any equity or payment terms. The kit prepares material; the company and its lawyer decide terms.

## Steps

1. **State the purpose.** What decisions or gaps the advisers are for. Without a purpose, stop and ask the owner. Set a size (usually three to six) and a term (for example 12 months, renewable).
2. **Define roles.** For each adviser type: the gap they fill, what they will do, what they will not do (no authority to bind the company), how much time (for example two hours a month) and who in the company they report to. Add the chair (a company person who runs the meetings) and a secretary.
3. **Write expectations both ways.** Adviser: attendance, response time, honest views, confidentiality, declaring conflicts. Company: preparing papers in advance, sharing the information needed, acting on or explaining decisions, not misusing the adviser's name.
4. **Write a terms summary in plain language** for a lawyer to review. Cover: purpose and term, time commitment, confidentiality, conflicts of interest, intellectual property from the adviser's input (who owns ideas shared), no authority to bind, independence from the company's directors, termination, any payment or equity (state "to be decided by the company with legal advice; none offered here"), data protection, and use of name and likeness. Label it **draft, not legal advice**, and list the questions for the lawyer. Do not offer or imply equity or fees.
5. **Set the meeting rhythm.** Frequency (for example quarterly with a short monthly check-in), a standard agenda (decisions needed, progress since last time, one deep topic, actions), papers circulated a set number of days before, notes within a few days, and an action log.
6. **Make a contribution map.** For each adviser: the area, the specific help asked, evidence of why them (what the owner knows, with source), the first contribution requested and the date, and how the company will know the contribution helped. Mark anything unverified. Do not describe an adviser's credentials beyond what the owner has verified.
7. **Write the public-statements rules.** What advisers may say (that they advise the company, in a stated role, once the company has agreed the wording), what they may not (claims about results, endorsements implying approval of products, confidential information, anything suggesting they speak for the company), and how to get wording approved. Check with each adviser before any name is listed publicly.
8. **Stop.** Do not send invitations, propose terms to advisers, announce names or sign anything.

## Output

Use [template.md](template.md): purpose, roles, expectations, terms summary with lawyer questions, meeting rhythm, contribution map and public-statements rules. Save with the project, for example `.work/advisory/kit.md`.

## Checks

- A purpose, size and term are stated.
- Roles say what advisers cannot do as well as what they do.
- The terms summary is labelled draft and not legal advice, and lists questions for a lawyer.
- No equity or payment is offered.
- The contribution map marks unverified facts and has a first request per adviser.
- Public-statement rules require approval of wording; no names announced.
